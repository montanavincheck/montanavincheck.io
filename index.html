Which States Are Cracking Down on Montana Plates

You've seen it. A Lamborghini in Beverly Hills, a Ford F-250 in suburban Chicago, a brand-new RV in a Salt Lake City driveway, and every one of them wearing Montana plates.

That's no coincidence. It's the "Montana loophole," and in 2026 the states losing money on it have stopped looking the other way. Here's how it works, which states are going after it, and what to check if you're buying a car with Montana tags.

How the Montana loophole works

Montana has no statewide sales tax and very low registration fees. It also lets non-residents form an LLC there. So an owner in another state forms a Montana LLC, has the LLC buy the vehicle, and registers it under the company's Montana address. Registered agents will handle the whole thing for as little as $1,000, and the owner never sets foot in Montana.

On paper, the car belongs to a Montana business. In reality, it lives in a garage a thousand miles away.

Here's the catch. Forming the LLC isn't what gets people in trouble. The trouble starts when the car is primarily driven and stored in the owner's home state. That state can still claim sales or use tax, registration fees, and emissions compliance. 🚩

California: the biggest crackdown so far

California has been the loudest. Its tax agency (CDTFA) and DMV announced the results of a three-year campaign in early March 2026:

  • About 500 dealers tied to more than 2,500 sales involving Montana registrations
  • 14 people charged by the state attorney general in a 57-count complaint, including conspiracy to commit tax evasion, false sales tax returns, money laundering, and perjury
  • Roughly $4 million in unpaid taxes and fees recovered
  • Investigators finding some dealers filing sham shipping documents so customers could drive off the lot on Montana plates

The state also uses automated license plate readers to flag Montana-plated cars that keep showing up on California roads, then compares the sightings against registration and tax records.

Reporting this fall says California went further. A new law, SB 1406, took effect at the end of September 2026. As described in coverage, it targets residents who use out-of-state LLCs for vehicle registration and can hold LLC members liable for the taxes owed. Because it's so new, how it will be enforced is still taking shape.

Utah: letters, then payments

Utah went after Montana registrations with a notification campaign. It started by contacting 1,608 vehicle owners, and the state reported collecting $1.6 million in taxes plus about $32,500 in penalties from 558 residents. Reporting this September said Utah's analysis points to as many as 80,000 vehicles in play, so the effort is far from finished.

Illinois, Indiana, Georgia, and Missouri

Several other states have moved through new laws:

  • Illinois passed legislation that lets the state "look through" the LLC and hold the individual resident liable for unpaid vehicle-related taxes. That undercuts the main selling point of the structure.
  • Indiana passed legislation aimed at improper out-of-state registrations, including Montana LLC setups.
  • Georgia is putting enforcement tools in place under legislation passed in 2025.
  • Missouri had a bill moving earlier this year that would bar residents from registering vehicles elsewhere to avoid Missouri taxes, with penalties that could include fines and license suspension. Check its current status, since proposed bills change.

Other states on the list

Iowa, Massachusetts, and Illinois have pursued Montana-registered vehicles for years. Tennessee made national news with criminal tax evasion indictments against the YouTuber known as WhistlinDiesel, who posted a video destroying a Montana-plated Ferrari. Some coverage also names Wyoming among the states stepping up, though the strongest details I found were for the states above.

In short: California, Utah, Illinois, Indiana, Georgia, and Tennessee are the clearest examples, with Missouri and others in the pipeline.

How states catch it

You don't have to be doing anything flashy to get noticed. States use a mix of tools:

  • License plate readers on police cars, toll gantries, and parking lots, logging how often a Montana plate appears in the same city
  • Dealer records showing sales to local buyers who claimed the car was headed to Montana
  • Data matching across DMV, tax, insurance, and property records
  • Tips and traffic stops that lead to questions about who actually owns and drives the vehicle

A Montana plate that never leaves your neighborhood is a pattern, and patterns show up in data. 📡

What it means if you're buying a used car

This is where it hits you as a buyer, not just a tax story. A car with Montana plates may carry extra baggage:

  • Title and registration trouble. If the state decides the registration was improper, you may face back taxes, penalties, or a hold on re-registering the car.
  • Unclear ownership. The LLC is the owner on paper. Make sure the person selling has real authority to sell, and ask for the LLC paperwork.
  • A seller who can't explain the history. If they dodge questions about where the car has actually been driven, treat that as a warning.
  • Emissions and inspection gaps. A car that skipped your state's requirements may not pass when you try to register it at home.
  • Re-registration costs. Expect to pay your state's sales or use tax and fees when you title the car in your own name, and ask the DMV in advance what they'll require.

Do the basics too: match the VIN, run a history report, check for liens, and get an independent mechanic's inspection.

If you already have a Montana LLC registration

I'm not a lawyer or tax advisor, and the rules vary by state, so treat this as general information. If you live in one of these states and use a Montana-registered vehicle there, it's worth talking to a tax attorney or CPA. Some states have offered voluntary disclosure programs that can reduce penalties for people who come forward before they're contacted, so ask about that early.

If you genuinely live in Montana or keep the vehicle out of your home state, the picture is different. Stay organized, keep records, and get professional advice about your specific situation.

The bottom line

The Montana plate used to be a quiet shortcut. In 2026, it's a flag. California, Utah, Illinois, Indiana, Georgia, and Tennessee are all pushing back, with criminal charges in some cases and new laws that reach past the LLC to the person behind it. If you're buying a car with Montana tags, ask more questions than usual and verify everything before you pay.

A little extra homework now beats a surprise tax bill later.
